# ACHU BUSINESS HUB — REAL GAME REBUILD

## Core premise
You start with a tiny cleaning business, almost no money, one vehicle, basic equipment and no reputation. The city does not care who you are yet. You must turn enquiries into quotes, quotes into jobs, jobs into reviews, reviews into recurring clients, and recurring clients into a real company.

The game is not about levelling abstract buildings. It is about surviving and growing a service business.

## The player fantasy
From one person doing small domestic jobs to running crews, vehicles, recurring routes and major commercial contracts across the city.

## Core loop
1. A lead appears from a real client or business.
2. Read what they actually need: property type, size, urgency, expectations and budget pressure.
3. Decide whether to accept, decline or quote.
4. Price the job. Too high can lose it. Too low can win work that loses money.
5. Assign a crew, vehicle, supplies and time slot.
6. The job runs. Traffic, lateness, missing supplies, crew skill and client expectations can affect the result.
7. Get paid, receive feedback/review, gain or lose reputation.
8. Use profit to improve equipment, hire people, buy/lease vehicles, rent better premises or improve systems.
9. Better reputation unlocks better leads and commercial contracts.
10. Expand only when the current operation can handle it.

## The story
### Chapter 1 — One chance
ACHU begins with a small amount of cash, basic tools and one vehicle. The first client is not a tutorial button: they are a named person with a problem and a deadline. Complete the job well enough to earn the first review.

### Chapter 2 — Word gets around
A second client arrives because of the first review. The player learns that reputation is more valuable than raw XP. A bad quote, late arrival or weak service can now have consequences.

### Chapter 3 — Too much work for one person
Three enquiries overlap. The player cannot do everything. This forces the first real decision: reject work, delay it, or hire help. Unlock the first employee only because the business genuinely needs one.

### Chapter 4 — The first complaint
A job goes wrong or a client complains. The player chooses how to respond: refund, partial refund, free revisit, defend the work, or absorb the reputation hit. This introduces quality control and customer retention.

### Chapter 5 — The first van
The existing vehicle becomes a bottleneck. The player can buy/lease a better van. Vehicles change capacity, travel reliability and operating cost. They are not decorative units.

### Chapter 6 — A proper base
Home/garage operations become inefficient. Rent a small storage unit or office. Premises unlock because they solve a real operational problem: storage, dispatch, staff capacity, laundry/equipment, admin.

### Chapter 7 — Commercial work
A local office/restaurant/property manager offers a recurring contract. Commercial work pays more but has service-level expectations, repeat schedules and penalties for failure.

### Chapter 8 — Competition
Other cleaning companies begin appearing in quotes and tenders. Competition is not combat. The player competes on price, rating, response speed, capacity and specialist capability.

### Chapter 9 — Scale or break
More work creates scheduling, supply and management pressure. Systems matter now: CRM, route planning, stock control, training and supervision. Tech upgrades exist because the player feels the pain they solve.

### Chapter 10 — The company
Multiple crews, vehicles, recurring contracts and districts. The player decides whether to remain a premium local operator or scale aggressively into a regional business.

## What replaces the old 4X logic
- Army -> Staff and crew capacity
- Troop power -> Operational capability, calculated from people, skill, equipment, vehicles and management
- Battles -> Competitive tenders / difficult jobs / service challenges
- Settlements -> Real client locations and commercial sites
- Resource nodes -> Actual leads, suppliers, recruitment candidates and opportunities
- Territory capture -> Service coverage generated by recurring clients and route economics
- Marches -> Crew dispatches with travel time and schedules
- Furnace/HQ levels -> Premises progression: home base -> storage unit -> small office -> depot -> regional HQ

## Player-visible resources
Keep the economy understandable:
- Cash — money available now
- Reputation — client trust and public rating
- Supplies — consumables/equipment stock
- Capacity — staff-hours available today

Talent is not a passive resource. Candidates are people with salary, skill, reliability and availability.

## Clients
Every meaningful job should belong to a client, not an anonymous tile.
Client data can include:
- name / business name
- domestic or commercial
- property/site type
- frequency
- size
- preferred day/time
- difficulty
- price sensitivity
- quality expectation
- reliability
- lifetime value
- satisfaction history

Recurring clients should remain visible in the world and schedule.

## Quoting
A quote is a real decision.
Player sees expected labour, supplies, travel and risk. The game can suggest a range but does not choose for the player.
Outcome chance depends on price, reputation, response time, specialist capability and competition.

## Jobs
Jobs should show a timeline: travel -> arrival -> service -> inspection -> payment/review.
Events can alter outcomes: traffic, no access, extra rooms, damaged equipment, staff sickness, client changes scope, supply shortage.

## Staff
Named employees with traits and progression. Example attributes:
- speed
- quality
- reliability
- specialist skills
- wage
- morale
- availability

No faceless troop batches.

## Vehicles
Named physical assets with:
- seats
- equipment capacity
- running cost
- reliability
- speed/travel modifier
- lease/payment
- condition

## Premises
Premises are milestone upgrades, not twelve arbitrary levels.
1. Home base
2. Storage unit
3. Small office
4. Operations depot
5. Regional HQ

Each one has a visual identity and solves a capacity problem the player has already experienced.

## Systems / research
No generic tech tree at the start. Systems unlock when a pain exists.
Examples:
- Route Planner: reduces travel waste after multiple jobs overlap.
- CRM: improves follow-up and recurring-client conversion after enough leads exist.
- Stock Control: reduces supply failures once stock complexity appears.
- Training Programme: improves new-hire quality after first employees are hired.
- Quality Checklist: reduces complaints after the first quality incident.
- Automated Reminders: reduces no-access / forgotten appointment risk.

The UI must always say the exact before -> after effect.

## City design
The city is not a 21x21 board.
Use a small authored city with 4-6 distinct districts. Roads and blocks are permanent. Client buildings exist at real addresses/locations in that fictional city. New leads appear inside these districts.

District examples:
- Residential suburb — domestic recurring work
- Town centre — apartments, shops, offices
- Business park — commercial contracts
- Industrial estate — warehouses and specialist cleaning
- Premium district — high expectations, high margins

## First 10 minutes
Minute 0-1: Player sees a believable small base and receives a message/call from the first client.
Minute 1-2: Open lead. Understand the property and what the client needs.
Minute 2-3: Make the first quote with a clear suggested cost/margin range.
Minute 3-4: Client accepts. Schedule the job.
Minute 4-6: Van visibly drives to the client. Job progress is shown in the world and UI.
Minute 6-7: Result: payment + client reaction + first review.
Minute 7-8: Review creates a second lead.
Minute 8-10: Player must choose between spending profit on supplies/equipment or keeping cash. The next client appears naturally.

The player should understand the game without opening a manual.

## Visual direction
Target: modern stylised PBR, not prototype low-poly.
- believable road widths, curbs, pavements and parking
- real building proportions
- textured glass, brick, concrete, metal and signage
- proper shadows, ambient occlusion/light baking where possible
- restrained colour palette with ACHU green only as an accent
- modern vans and cars with rotating wheels and traffic
- people at believable scale
- day/night and weather later, only after the daytime scene is strong

## Asset policy
Do not reuse primitive cube buildings as final visuals.
Do not use obviously dated prototype packs as primary city art.
Prefer modern downloadable GLB/FBX assets with clear commercial-compatible licensing, PBR/baked textures and reasonable poly counts. Record creator, source and licence for every external asset.

Reference quality discovered during research:
- modern office building models with baked/game-ready textures and CC Attribution
- 2025 delivery van models with game-ready geometry and CC Attribution
- current stylised PBR urban prop packs for street furniture/details

## UI rule
At any moment the main screen answers only four questions:
1. What needs my attention now?
2. What will happen if I do it?
3. What will it cost / earn?
4. What problem is blocking me?

No six-menu dashboard at minute one. Features appear when the player needs them.

## Non-negotiable test
Give the game to someone with no explanation. After 60 seconds they must be able to answer:
- who they are
- what the company does
- what they need to do next
- why they should do it
- what they are trying to become

If they cannot, the build fails regardless of graphics.
